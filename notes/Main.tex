\noindent
\textbf{Author:} Røskva Bjørgfinsdóttir\\
\textbf{Title:} Sleeping patterns and flexible minds: Disentangling the effects of habitual and nightly sleep on cognitive control\\
\textbf{Supervisor:} Christina Thunberg \\
\textbf{Co-supervisor:} René Huster
\vspace{0.5cm}

Sleep is important for cognitive functioning, but it is still unclear whether naturalistic variation in sleep relates differently to different components of cognitive control. This thesis examined whether habitual sleep duration, sleep variability, and night-before sleep deviation were associated with behavioural and neural indices of cognitive control in a task-switching paradigm. The study used data from the Mind the Sleep project. Behavioural and EEG task effects were examined in 50 adults, while sleep-related mixed-effects models were restricted to participants with complete sleep data (\(N = 46\)). Sleep was assessed using daily sleep diaries. Cognitive flexibility was operationalised as switch costs, and cognitive stability as congruency effects. Frontal midline theta power was extracted from EEG as mean 4--8 Hz event-related spectral perturbation power from 200--500 ms after stimulus onset, averaged across Fz, FCz, and Cz.

The task produced clear behavioural effects. Participants were slower and less accurate on switch trials than repeat trials, and less accurate on incongruent than congruent trials. Reaction time and accuracy also showed switch \(\times\) congruency interactions, indicating that the task successfully manipulated both switching and interference demands. In contrast, theta power did not show reliable modulation by switch, congruency, or their interaction. Sleep-related mixed-effects models showed nominal interactions between sleep variables and switch condition for several outcomes. Longer-than-usual sleep before testing and greater habitual sleep variability were associated with smaller reaction time switch costs. Habitual sleep duration and sleep variability were also nominally associated with switch-related accuracy and theta differences, but in opposite directions. No sleep-related fixed effects survived family-wise error rate correction, and no consistent sleep-related effects were observed for congruency.

Overall, the findings provide strong behavioural evidence for switch and congruency costs in the task, but only tentative evidence that naturalistic sleep variation relates to cognitive control. The sleep-related effects appeared more often for switch-related outcomes than for congruency-related outcomes. Frontal midline theta did not function as a clear marker of task demand in the present analysis. These results suggest that associations between everyday sleep and cognitive control may be more complex and weaker than expected, and should be interpreted cautiously. Larger samples, repeated EEG sessions, and more detailed sleep measurement are needed to clarify whether habitual and nightly sleep variation differentially affect cognitive flexibility and stability. \newline



\textbf{Keywords:} sleep, executive function, cognitive control, task switching

\chapter{Introduction}

Sleep is a fundamental biological process that supports cognitive and emotional functioning. Even in healthy adults, sleep duration and quality will differ across individuals and across time, shaped by circadian rhythms, lifestyle factors, and environmental demands \parencite{foster2020sleep}. Habitual sleep patterns may reflect relatively stable individual differences, whereas nightly deviations can reflect transient changes in arousal, fatigue, and regulatory capacity \parencite{fjell2024sleep}. Understanding how these different aspects of sleep relate to cognition is important because everyday performance often depends on long-term sleep habits as well as sleep quality in the days leading up to a demanding situation.

There is an extensive body of research linking sleep and executive function, where two conceptual gaps are relevant for the present thesis. First, it is unclear how robust the effects of sleep on cognitive control are, and whether findings from controlled sleep deprivation studies extend to more naturalistic sleep. Much of the literature relies on acute manipulations of sleep loss, and it remains uncertain to what extent these findings generalise to functioning in daily life. Secondly, cognitive control is not a unitary construct. Different components, such as stability and flexibility, may be affected in different ways. It is therefore unclear whether sleep has similar or distinct effects across these aspects of control. This also raises the question of whether different aspects of sleep, such as continuity and variability, modulate these components in the same way. Even when studies use objective and ecologically valid measures of sleep, fewer distinguish between habitual sleep level, how a person typically sleeps over days or weeks, and sleep variability, meaning how much sleep duration fluctuates from night to night. Habitual sleep may reflect more stable characteristics, whereas variability may capture instability in sleep timing and quality that is relevant for cognitive control \parencite{fjell2024sleep}. This distinction can potentially influence our understanding of whether stable sleep patterns and night-to-night fluctuations relate differently to cognitive control.



\section{Stability and flexibility}

Executive functions are processes that support higher-order thinking such as reasoning, planning, problem solving and interacting with novel situations. They allow us to regulate thoughts and actions in accordance with internal goals. The three most studied executive functions are inhibition, working memory updating and set shifting \parencite{friedman2017unity,diamond2013executive}. Two of these functions, updating and shifting, are separable, with inhibition largely captured by a common domain-general function \parencite{friedman2017unity}. 

In the present thesis we will focus on the function of set shifting, also known as task switching, which can be understood as the process of disengaging from a previously relevant task set and activating a new one \parencite{rogers1995taskswitch, monsell2003switching, kiesel2010review}. In daily life, cognitive control often requires a balance between flexibility and stability. Flexibility is needed when the relevant goal changes, such as when we stop looking at a phone and shift attention to a person speaking to us, or when a driver stops following the planned route and adapts to an unexpected hazard. Stability is needed when the current goal must be maintained despite competing information, such as continuing to follow a conversation in a noisy room or keeping attention on traffic while irrelevant stimuli compete for attention. Failures of flexibility can make behaviour rigid or slow to adapt, and failures of stability can make behaviour distractible or vulnerable to interference

\paragraph{The classical view} According to \textcite{egner2023principles} the classical cognitive control literature can be divided into two different traditions of how to understand these processes. Studies on conflict control focus on the goal-shielding aspect of task switching, how we focus on the information relevant to the task and ignore irrelevant stimuli \parencite{botvinick2001conflict}. The other tradition is more concerned with the aspect of shifting focus, or task switching, when we are cued to do so \parencite{monsell2003switching}. The conflict control-tradition roughly translates to stability, and the task switching aspect translates to flexibility. Traditionally, these have often been treated as competing demands, where stronger maintenance of a task set can make it harder to switch away from it, and greater readiness to switch can make the current task set more vulnerable to interference \parencite{goschke2000intentional, geddert2022tradeoff}. This has later been described as a unidimensional trade-off, where increased flexibility comes at the cost of reduced stability and vice versa \parencite{geddert2022tradeoff,nack2023metacognition}.

A classical way of studying conflict control is by using Stroop paradigms, where participants are presented with a list of colour words and have to report the colour of the ink instead of reading the words \parencite{stroop1935interference}. The idea is that there will be a conflict between the dominant task set, which is reading the word, and the task-relevant colour-naming response. In this task, the stimuli can be either congruent or incongruent. When the written word is the same as the ink colour, the stimulus is congruent because it will be the same no matter which task set you have activated. When the colour of the ink is different than the written word, the stimulus is incongruent because there is a conflict of competing demands. Participants will usually show a congruence effect in the form of either reduced accuracy or prolonged reaction time for incongruent trials compared to congruent trials \parencite{macleod1991half,parris2022loci}.

Contextual factors can influence the size of both congruency effects and switch costs. Congruency effects refer to poorer performance on incongruent than congruent trials, whereas switch costs refer to poorer performance on switch trials than repeat trials. In Stroop-like conflict tasks, congruency effects are often smaller when conflicting trials are frequent than when they are rare. For example, \textcite{logan1979misled} showed that participants adjusted their attention depending on how often the relevant and irrelevant stimulus dimensions conflicted, suggesting that interference effects are shaped by the task environment rather than only by the stimulus itself. This type of context-dependent adjustment has later been described as list-wide proportion congruency effects, where the overall frequency of conflict changes the size of the congruency effect, and item-specific proportion congruency effects, where interference is reduced for items that are more often associated with conflict \parencite{bugg2012support,bugg2012dissociating}.

Similar context effects have been found in task-switching paradigms. List-wide proportion switch effects refer to the finding that switch costs are generally smaller in blocks with many switch trials than in blocks with few switch trials \parencite{siqiliu2020contextual,dreisbach2006preparatory,schneider2006priming}. Item-specific proportion switch effects show a similar pattern at the item level, where switch costs are reduced for stimuli or cues that are more often associated with switching \parencite{chiu2017cueing,kang2021proactive}. The list-wide and item-specific effects suggest that participants can learn regularities in control demands over time and use this information to adjust how control is allocated \parencite{egner2023principles}. In this sense, the size of switch costs and congruency effects may also reflect contextual influences such as whether the environment favours updating, preparation, or stronger task-set maintenance

The list-wide and item-specific effects have been interpreted as context-dependent shifts along a single stability--flexibility dimension \parencite{goschke2000intentional, geddert2022tradeoff, dreisbach2019flexible, egner2023principles}. The reasoning is that if the current task set is strongly protected, it should resist both distraction and updating. This supports stability, but makes flexible switching more difficult. Conversely, if the system is more open to updating, switching becomes easier, but irrelevant task information can intrude more easily. When conceptualised in this way, stability and flexibility end up in a trade-off against each other \parencite{dreisbach2012mechanisms, dreisbach2019flexible}. Following this reasoning, behavioural indices of stability and flexibility should show opposite patterns. High stability should be associated with reduced congruency effects, because the current task set is better shielded from irrelevant information, but with increased switch costs, because the system is less open to updating. High flexibility should be associated with reduced switch costs, but with larger congruency effects, because irrelevant task information may intrude more easily. Empirical evidence for this position comes from task switching experiments with bivalent stimuli \parencite{rogers1995taskswitch, kiesel2010review}. When a stimulus is bivalent, it means that it can map on to two different rules, depending on the cue. For example, in the article by \textcite{rogers1995taskswitch}, they introduced the task that was used in the current thesis (the paradigm is described in more detail in the Methods-section). A letter-number pair is displayed to the participants, and depending on the currently active rule, they must decide whether the number is odd or even, or whether the letter is a consonant or a vowel. The participants respond with a left or right button press (e.g. the correct response for a number being odd can be a right button press, and the correct response for a letter being a consonant can be a right button press). If the correct button press response is the same whether you get the number task or the letter task, the trial is congruent. If the correct response is different depending on which task you get, the trial is incongruent. In these types of paradigms, participants typically perform worse on switch trials than on repeat trials, which may partly stem from interference from the irrelevant task set still being active \parencite{rogers1995taskswitch, monsell2003switching, kiesel2010review}. We also typically observe slower reaction times and lower accuracy on incongruent trials, and the effect of switching is enhanced when the trial is also incongruent \parencite{rogers1995taskswitch, kiesel2010review}. This has been interpreted as consistent with the lower updating threshold needed for switching also resulting in less shielding of the active rule, and thus more interference from the irrelevant task set \parencite{dreisbach2012mechanisms, dreisbach2019flexible}.

Increased switch costs are also seen in trials following incongruent trials, possibly because more stability is necessary in trials with conflict, with carry-over effects to the next trial indicating a trade-off \parencite{botvinick2001conflict, goschke2000intentional, kiesel2010review}. Affect and reward manipulations also tend to produce opposite effects on stability and flexibility, where positive affect has been linked to more flexible responding, but also to weaker maintenance of task cues or increased distractibility \parencite{dreisbach2004positive, goschke2014emotional}. In other words, pleasant stimuli may make it easier to react to changing task demands, but at the cost of poorer shielding of the currently relevant task set. Reward prospect has shown the opposite pattern. \textcite{hefer2017reward} conducted three experiments using different versions of the AX-continuous performance task, where participants maintain an earlier cue to respond correctly to a later target. Reward increased reliance on the cue, suggesting greater cognitive stability, but this also made participants slower to adapt when cues became less useful, suggesting reduced flexibility. In an experiment by \textcite{braem2017conditioning}, participants were shown to choose switching tasks more often in a voluntary switching paradigm where switch trials were rewarded. This indicated increased flexibility, but participants also showed larger cross-task congruency effects, suggesting greater interference from the irrelevant task set.

Even though there is a body of literature supporting the view of a stability-flexibility trade-off, the story may be more nuanced. \textcite{egner2023principles} criticises the traditional view both on conceptual and empirical grounds. He argues that a unidimensional take on stability and flexibility with an inverse relationship between the two constructs renders the hypothesis unfalsifiable because it means we cannot manipulate or test them separately. If there is only one dimension, increasing switch readiness should necessarily increase interference, and increasing task focus should necessarily impair switching. However, empirical studies have shown that this is not always the case, suggesting that stability and flexibility can sometimes be regulated independently rather than being locked into a single trade-off dimension. 



\paragraph{Introducing a new dimension} Contemporary frameworks contend that flexibility and stability are not necessarily opposite ends of a single scale, but could instead reflect coordinated processes that regulate when to maintain a goal and when to update it \parencite{egner2023principles, nack2023metacognition}. \textcite{nack2023metacognition} make a case for a dual-dimensional view of set shifting within a framework of metacontrol, defined as context-dependent regulation of cognitive control. Within this framework, flexibility and stability are conceptualised as metacontrol states instead of fixed capacities. They argue that manipulations of flexibility within participants do not consistently produce corresponding changes in stability. For example, increasing the frequency of task switches reliably reduces switch costs, indicating increased flexibility, but does not necessarily increase congruency effects, which would be expected if stability were reduced \parencite{geddert2022tradeoff}. Similar dissociations have been reported when flexibility is manipulated through training or affect, suggesting that improved switching performance does not always come at the cost of greater susceptibility to interference \parencite{zhao2020switchtraining,sali2021flexibleattention}. These findings are difficult to reconcile with a strict trade-off account.

Evidence from individual-differences studies raises a related question. If flexibility and stability were strictly opposed, individuals with higher flexibility should consistently show lower stability. Instead, individual differences often affect one component without systematically influencing the other. For instance, factors such as bilingual experience, or emotion regulation can improve switching performance without altering interference effects \parencite{christoeffels2015bilingualflexible, mcrae2012reappraisalcontrol}. Conversely, some factors affect stability without changing flexibility. When asked, participants will often say they are either good or bad at both shifting attention and keeping focus, and they rarely see these skills as needing to be traded off against each other \parencite{derryberry2002anxiouscontrol}. Factor analyses have also shown that stability and flexibility load onto two factors and not one, meaning that they seem to reflect partly independent processes \parencite{bejjani2022latentswitch, chiorri2017factoranalysis}.

Common situations in daily life also frequently point to a potential independence between stability and flexibility, where increasing one does not necessarily mean decreasing the other. To illustrate, a fairly typical context of making dinner is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:controlstates}. Here stability is illustrated as the ability to maintain the currently relevant task, like chopping vegetables, without being distracted by irrelevant stimuli. Flexibility is illustrated as the ability to update behaviour when task demands change, like shifting attention to the pot before it boils over. In the high-stability/low-flexibility example, the woman remains focused on chopping carrots, but fails to respond to the pot boiling over. In the low-stability/high-flexibility example, she responds to the pot, but is easily pulled away from the current task. These two examples map onto the traditional trade-off view, where high stability implies low flexibility and high flexibility implies low stability. The lower panels illustrate the additional possibilities allowed by a dual-dimensional view. In the high-stability/high-flexibility example, she remains focused on the relevant task while still updating behaviour when the pot requires attention. In the low-stability/low-flexibility example, she is distracted by irrelevant stimuli and also fails to shift attention to the pot in time. This illustrates how stability and flexibility can be conceptualised as partly independent dimensions rather than as opposite ends of one scale.

\paragraph{Measuring stability and flexibility} In the present thesis, stability and flexibility are operationalised through behavioural indices rather than treated as direct measurements of isolated cognitive processes. Flexibility is indexed by switch costs, defined as poorer performance on switch trials than repeat trials, whereas stability is indexed by congruency effects, defined as poorer performance on incongruent than congruent trials. Task performance is measured using accuracy and reaction times. Because the task used here combines switch/repeat and congruent/incongruent conditions, both indices can be examined within the same paradigm. The task itself is described in detail in the Methods section.



\section{Electrophysiological correlates}

Electroencephalography (EEG) is commonly used to investigate cognitive conflict and task switching \parencite{luck2014erp, cohen2013thetaconflict, cavanagh2015metatheta, cavanagh2014theta, depestele2023thetadriving}. It is a non-invasive and cost-effective method for measuring electrical activity in the brain. EEG does not measure individual action potentials, but reflects the summed postsynaptic potentials of large populations of active neurons in the cortex. These signals propagate through brain tissue, cerebrospinal fluid, skull, and scalp before being detected by electrodes on the scalp. As a result, EEG has high temporal resolution but relatively low spatial resolution. This allows us to track the timing of neural processes with millisecond precision, even though the exact spatial sources of the signals are less precisely localised \parencite[Chapter 3]{gazzaniga2025principles}. 

At the neural level, task switching and cognitive control rely on distributed frontoparietal networks involving medial and lateral prefrontal regions as well as posterior parietal cortex \parencite{kiesel2010review}. These regions are implicated in maintaining task goals, allocating attention, and resolving interference when behaviour must be adjusted to changing task demands. Cognitive control has traditionally been studied in the time-domain using event-related potentials (ERPs), including the N2 component, a negative deflection around 200--300 ms after stimulus onset that has been associated with conflict monitoring and cognitive control \parencite{folstein2008n2,luck2014erp}. Control related neural activity can also be captured in the time-frequency domain by increases in midfrontal theta-band power (FM-theta, 4–8 Hz), which can reflect oscillatory dynamics that are not necessarily phase-locked across trials \parencite{cavanagh2014theta,cavanagh2015metatheta,cohen2013thetaconflict}. Event-related oscillatory activity can be distinguished into evoked and induced components. Evoked activity is phase-locked to stimulus onset and therefore preserved in the averaged ERP, whereas induced activity varies in phase across trials and largely cancels out during averaging \parencite{herrmann2014timefrequencytutorial,herrmann2005waveletanalysis}. As a result, ERP analyses primarily reflect phase-locked responses and may underestimate oscillatory dynamics that are not strictly time-locked. Time–frequency analysis is an alternative that is applied at the single-trial level prior to averaging, allowing both evoked and induced activity to be captured in measures of total activity, often referred to as event-related spectral perturbation (ERSP).

Time–frequency analyses have shown that theta-band power can persist after removal of the ERP, suggesting partially distinct neural processes \parencite{herrmann2014timefrequencytutorial}, with theta activity being more closely linked to behavioral performance \parencite{cohen2013thetaconflict}. Converging evidence from EEG and intracranial recordings suggests that FM-theta is primarily generated in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and adjacent medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), regions implicated in performance monitoring and the dynamic allocation of control \parencite{cavanagh2014theta,depestele2023thetadriving}. FM-theta is thought to index a domain-general control signal that increases when ongoing behaviour requires adjustment \parencite{cavanagh2014theta,cavanagh2015metatheta}. Accordingly, FM-theta power is enhanced under conditions that place higher demands on control, including response conflict, error commission, and task switching. Switch trials and incongruent trials often elicit stronger theta activity than low-conflict conditions, suggesting that medial frontal systems register the need for increased control when competing task sets or response tendencies are activated \parencite{cohen2013thetaconflict, cavanagh2014theta}. 


\section{Stable ability or context sensitive}

Cognitive control can be studied both as a relatively stable individual-difference construct and as a context-sensitive process. Behavioural and electrophysiological measures of inhibition often show limited temporal stability in empirical studies, suggesting that situational and state-like factors contribute to task performance \parencite{thunberg2024unreliability,fassbender2023lst}. 
\textcite{thunberg2024unreliability} tested whether common stop-signal task measures behave like stable individual-difference measures by examining behavioural, EEG, and EMG variables across repeated sessions in three independent samples. Their results showed a clear distinction between different types of measures. Variables that directly described observable task behaviour, such as stop-signal delays and reaction times on go-trials, were relatively stable across sessions, with average test–retest correlations above .70. By contrast, the stop-signal reaction time, which is often used as an estimate of stopping latency, was much less stable over time. Across the three studies, the average test–retest reliability of the SSRT was only r = .34, and none of the individual studies showed reliability above r = .55. Since the results could not be explained by poor split-half reliability or by measurement-error attenuation the paper argues that common inhibition measures appear to be influenced more by state-like and situational determinants than by stable trait-like ability.

Different methods can be used to study different aspects of cognitive control. Performance in control paradigms can be dynamically adjusted according to current demands and recent experience, including conflict, errors, switch probability, and contextual regularities \parencite{egner2023principles,bejjani2022latentswitch,cavanagh2014theta}. At the same time, individual-differences and repeated-measures studies suggest that cognitive-control performance also contains relatively stable person-level components \parencite{friedman2017unity,friedman2011genetic,fassbender2023lst}. The present thesis focuses on the state-sensitive side of this distinction by examining sleep as a potential contextual factor that may modulate cognitive control.


\section{Sleep as a potential contextual moderator}

Sleep varies both between individuals and from night to night within the same individual \parencite{bei2016sleepvariability}. This variation is shaped biologically by circadian and homeostatic regulation \parencite{foster2020sleep}, and by environmental demands, social schedules, and individual sleep need \parencite{fjell2024sleep}. Insufficient or disrupted sleep has been related to a host of negative outcomes for both mental and physical health \parencite{foster2020sleep}, and there is a large body of research linking sleep loss with impaired cognitive performance in domains like attention and executive functioning \parencite{lim2010meta, lowe2017meta, sen2023sleepef, tai2022sleepef, slama2018sleepswitching, wilckens2020sleepmddswitch, wilckens2014sleeppreparation, fjell2024sleep, kusztor2019differentialsleep, wilckens2016sleepcognitionage, basner2013sleepcognition, couyoumdjian2010switchingsleep}. 

Sleep can be measured both under controlled conditions, such as with sleep deprivation studies, or by measuring naturally occurring sleep variation, or a combination of both. In experimental sleep manipulation studies, researchers deliberately change sleep and then test cognition. This includes total sleep deprivation, where participants are kept awake for an extended period, often over 24 hours, and partial sleep restriction, where sleep is reduced to a few hours across one or more nights. Cognition is then tested either before, during, and/or after the manipulation. Experimental designs give stronger causal inference, but it is not known whether the artificial situations created generalize well to everyday sleep patterns \parencite{sen2023sleepef, fjell2024sleep}. 

Purely observational sleep studies offer a more naturalistic approach, relying on self-reported measures such as sleep duration, sleep quality scores, time spent in bed and nightly awakenings. The sleep data is then related to cognitive outcomes, brain measures and health outcomes, such as the risk of developing conditions like dementia or mental illness \parencite{sen2023sleepef, Shokri-Kojori2018beta-amyloid, alvaro2013sleepdepression}. In the UK Biobank, \textcite{tai2022sleepef} found that approximately seven hours of self-reported sleep was associated with the highest executive-function performance, measured with cognitive tasks, with performance decreasing below and above this duration. The quadratic relationship between sleep and cognitive performance suggests that more sleep is not necessarily better \parencite{sen2023sleepef,tai2022sleepef}. Observational studies of this magnitude can be useful for detecting population-level patterns, such as nonlinear associations between sleep duration and executive function. The limitations of these studies can be seen in self-report bias, confounding, and weaker causal inference than experimental designs \parencite{sen2023sleepef,tai2022sleepef}. A way to mitigate some of the limitations of cross-sectional studies is by using longitudinal designs, where sleep and cognition are measured repeatedly across time within the same participants. In their study, \textcite{sexton2014sleepatrophy} followed 147 community-dwelling adults with MRI scans separated by an average of 3.5 years and related longitudinal cortical change to self-reported sleep quality. Poor sleep quality has also been associated with greater cortical atrophy over time in widespread frontal, temporal, and parietal regions \parencite{sexton2014sleepatrophy}.

\paragraph{Measures of sleep} Sleep can be studied using both objective and subjective measures, depending on the design of the study and the aspects of sleep we hope to gain insights about. The most detailed method, which has also been dubbed the gold standard of sleep measurement, is polysomnography (PSG) \parencite{adamantidis2019oscillating}. It usually involves EEG and additional physiological recordings such as pulse and heart rate variability. This allows researchers to distinguish sleep stages such as REM and NREM sleep and to examine sleep architecture, slow-wave sleep, spindles, awakenings, and fragmentation. It gives a detailed picture of the mechanisms of sleep, but it is both expensive and intrusive because participants usually have to sleep in the lab for this kind of measurement. A more naturalistic objective method is actigraphy, where participants wear a movement-based device over several days or weeks. Actigraphy uses patterns of limb movement and immobility to estimate sleep and wakefulness, and can provide estimates of total sleep time, sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset, sleep efficiency, and restlessness \parencite{smith2018actigraphy_review, smith2018actigraphy_guideline}. However, because actigraphy infers sleep from movement rather than measuring sleep physiology directly, it cannot distinguish sleep stages in the same way as PSG and may misclassify quiet wakefulness as sleep. Compared with PSG, actigraphy tends to overestimate total sleep time and sleep efficiency, and underestimate sleep onset latency and wake after sleep onset \parencite{conley2019agreement}. Even though actigraphy is an objective measure, it is not a direct measure of sleep, and usually benefits from sleep diary information when defining the rest interval \parencite{smith2018actigraphy_review,smith2018actigraphy_guideline}. Direct comparison studies show that sleep diaries and actigraphy often agree reasonably well for sleep timing and total sleep time, but agreement is weaker for variables such as sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset, and sleep efficiency \parencite{campanini2017agreement,kearns2023agreement,zhu2018sleepdiaryactigraphy}.

A common subjective method for studying sleep is the use of questionnaires, such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index \parencite{buysse1989psqi}. The PSQI is a self-rated questionnaire that assesses sleep metrics and other factors such as the use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction over the previous month \parencite{sexton2014sleepatrophy}. Questionnaires are practical in large samples and can capture perceived sleep quality, but they depend on retrospective self-report and subjective interpretation, and may not correspond directly to objective sleep measures such as polysomnography \parencite{sexton2014sleepatrophy, tai2022sleepef}.
Sleep diaries are another common method for studying sleep in naturalistic settings because they can be completed repeatedly at home. Sleep diaries ask participants to report their sleep on a daily basis, usually in the morning shortly after waking, and can be used to estimate bedtime, attempted sleep time, sleep onset latency, nocturnal awakenings, wake after sleep onset, final awakening, total sleep time, sleep quality, and restedness \parencite{carney2012consensus}. Compared with retrospective questionnaires, daily sleep diaries reduce the time between the sleep episode and the report, but they remain subjective and depend on participants’ estimates of sleep and wakefulness. In Norway, the sleep diary provided by SOVno/Helse Bergen, developed by Bjørn Bjorvatn, is used in the assessment and treatment of sleep problems like insomnia and circadian rhythm disorders, and the Norwegian Directorate of Health refers to sleep-diary assessment in clinical follow-up of sleep problems \parencite{bjorvatn2025sovndagbok, helsedirektoratet2018sovnvansker}. Sleep diaries are also used in research, either alone or together with actigraphy, to capture naturalistic sleep patterns across days or weeks \parencite{liset2021sleep, marthinsen2024sleep}.



\paragraph{Differential effects}
Meta-analytic and review evidence suggests that the cognitive effects of sleep loss are more consistent for vigilance, attention, and processing speed than for broad measures of executive functioning \parencite{lim2010meta,lowe2017meta,killgore2010sleepcognition}. In their review, \textcite{killgore2010sleepcognition} argue that sleep loss reliably impairs alertness, vigilance, and response speed, while its effects on higher-level executive functions are more heterogeneous. Cognitive control tasks are not process-pure and often combine several processes within the same behavioural outcome. A slowing of reaction time or increase in errors after sleep loss can potentially reflect reduced vigilance or weaker goal maintenance, rather than a selective impairment of the nominal executive function that the task is meant to measure \parencite{killgore2010sleepcognition,kusztor2019differentialsleep,tucker2010sleepdeprivation}. \textcite{tucker2010sleepdeprivation} used a test battery designed specifically to separate executive and non-executive components of cognitive control tasks. They found that overall task performance was impaired, but some dissociated components, such as working-memory scanning efficiency and resistance to proactive interference, were preserved \parencite{tucker2010sleepdeprivation}. A similar component-level approach was taken by \textcite{kusztor2019differentialsleep} in their study examining whether acute sleep deprivation differentially affected components of cognitive control. They tested 24 participants in a within-subject design after a normal night of sleep at home, a night of sleep in the laboratory, and 24 hours of laboratory-controlled sleep deprivation. Participants performed a stop-signal task while EEG was recorded, allowing the researchers to distinguish sustained attention, response inhibition, error monitoring, and later top-down control processes. Sleep deprivation reduced go-trial accuracy and attenuated later ERP components such as the P300 and Pe, whereas stop-signal reaction time, the stop-related N200, and the ERN were comparatively preserved. In this experiment, acute sleep loss appeared to primarily disrupt sustained attention and effortful top-down control, while earlier inhibition- and monitoring-related indices were comparatively preserved \parencite{kusztor2019differentialsleep}.

The component-specific vulnerability of sleep effects on executive functions could potentially mean that sleep effects differ for stability and flexibility in task switching. \textcite{couyoumdjian2010switchingsleep} found that sleep deprivation impaired task-switching performance in 108 university students, with worse speed and accuracy on switch trials, and recovery after one night of sleep. Similarly, \textcite{slama2018sleepswitching} examined task switching and reported sleep-deprivation-related impairments in the form of higher switch costs. In a different task switching paradigm using bivalent stimuli, \textcite{heuer2004totalsleepdeprivation} studied the effects of one night of total sleep deprivation on shifting between simple cognitive tasks. Participants switched between numerical and spatial judgments, and the authors manipulated both task transitions and congruency between the response indicated by the relevant and irrelevant stimulus dimensions. Sleep deprivation increased reaction-time switch costs, with the strongest effects in the slow tail of the response-time distribution. The authors interpreted as evidence for greater task-set instability or lapsing under sleep deprivation. The increase in shift costs could not be explained by stronger congruency effects. In other words, sleep deprivation made switching more costly, but the effect did not appear to be primarily driven by increased interference between competing task sets \parencite{heuer2004totalsleepdeprivation}. A similar conclusion was reached by \textcite{bratzke2009constantwakefulness}, who tested task-switching performance repeatedly across 40 hours of constant wakefulness. Their paradigm also required participants to switch between numerical and spatial judgments, and included congruent and incongruent trials. Switch costs varied across the 40-hour protocol in a pattern consistent with both sleep homeostatic and circadian influences, indicating that task-switching efficiency was sensitive to prolonged wakefulness. Incongruency costs did not show the same systematic variation, and the authors concluded that the sleep- and circadian-related changes in switch costs were more likely to reflect impaired task preparation or task-set reconfiguration than increased automatic interference from irrelevant stimulus information \parencite{bratzke2009constantwakefulness}.

The literature suggests that sleep loss affects some cognitive control processes more strongly than others, like sustained attention, processing efficiency, task preparation, and effortful top-down control. In task-switching paradigms, this may affect both stability and flexibility, but possibly in different ways. Stability requires maintaining the currently relevant task rule and shielding it from competing stimulus information, which should be vulnerable if poor sleep weakens sustained attention or goal maintenance. Flexibility requires updating or reconfiguring the active task set when task demands change, which should be vulnerable if poor sleep impairs task preparation or increases task-set instability. The existing sleep and task-switching literature strongly suggests that sleep loss can increase switch costs, while the evidence for increased congruency-related interference is less consistent. The present study asks whether naturalistic sleep variation is differentially associated with behavioural and neural indices of flexibility, stability, or their interaction.

\paragraph{Decomposing naturalistic sleep} Mean sleep duration does not account for differences in how sleep is distributed across nights. \textcite{bei2016sleepvariability} argue that sleep--wake patterns should be understood in terms of both average levels and intraindividual variability, because night-to-night fluctuations constitute a meaningful dimension of sleep behaviour. Their review of 53 adult studies showed that sleep/wake variability is associated with demographic, psychological, behavioural, and health-related correlates, supporting the view that variability captures information not contained in mean sleep duration alone. Two participants may both average seven hours of sleep, but one may do so through stable sleep across nights, whereas the other may alternate between restricted and extended sleep. Treating these profiles as equivalent would ignore a potentially important aspect of naturalistic sleep \parencite{bei2016sleepvariability}.

Cognition has been studied using participants’ deviation from their own mean the night before testing. \textcite{gamaldo2010sleepcognition} examined within-person associations between daily sleep duration and cognitive performance in 50 community-dwelling adults across eight repeated assessments over a two- to three-week period. They found that greater deviation from an individual’s usual sleep duration was associated with poorer global cognitive performance, suggesting that sleep duration may be cognitively relevant as a within-person, state-like deviation from one’s own typical sleep. The same absolute sleep duration may represent normal sleep for one participant but an atypically short or long night for another \parencite{gamaldo2010sleepcognition}.

Naturalistic sleep can be usefully decomposed into habitual sleep, sleep variability, and night-before deviation. In everyday settings, sleep varies both within-individuals from night to night and between individuals in habitual sleep patterns \parencite{bei2016sleepvariability,fjell2024sleep}. These different sources of variation may have different cognitive meanings, because between-person differences and within-person fluctuations do not necessarily reflect the same process \parencite{hamaker2015critique}. Habitual sleep duration can captures relatively stable between-person differences, such as whether an individual generally sleeps more or less than others. Sleep variability captures the stability of sleep across nights, which may reflect irregular schedules, fluctuating sleep opportunity, or unstable sleep regulation \parencite{bei2016sleepvariability}. Night-before deviation captures a more state-like question, whether the participant slept more or less than usual immediately before cognitive testing. The same absolute sleep duration can mean different things for different people, where six hours of sleep may be typical for one participant but a large negative deviation for another \parencite{fjell2024sleep,yu2022priornight}. \textcite{fjell2024sleep} argue that experimentally restricted sleep is not necessarily a good model for understanding naturalistic sleep, because sleep need varies over time, differs between individuals, and depends on environmental, motivational, physiological, and social factors. Considering these natural variations, reducing sleep to a single metric of sleep duration may not capture the trait-like sleep patterns and state-dependent deviations that may influence the cognitive components we wish to study \parencite{fjell2024sleep}. 


\section{The current study}

The present thesis examines whether naturalistic variation in sleep is associated with behavioural and neural indices of cognitive control. Previous research has largely relied on acute sleep-deprivation paradigms, making it unclear whether similar patterns are observed when sleep varies naturally across days and individuals. In addition, cognitive control is not a unitary construct, and sleep-related effects may differ depending on which component of control is examined. The present study addresses these issues by linking repeated sleep-diary measures to behavioural and electrophysiological indices of cognitive control in a task-switching paradigm.

Since the present analyses focused primarily on total sleep time and person-centered deviations in total sleep time, diary-based sleep estimates were considered appropriate, while actigraphy was retained as an auxiliary validity check rather than as the primary analytic source. In the present thesis, diary-based measures were used to capture three complementary measures of total sleep time (TST).
Mean TST was used as an index of habitual sleep duration, reflecting how much a participant typically slept across valid diary nights in the 14-day period preceding the first EEG session. TST variability was used as an index of night-to-night fluctuation, reflecting how stable or irregular the participant's sleep duration was across the same 14-day period. TST deviation was used as a state-like measure of whether the participant slept more or less than their own average the night before testing. These complementary measures make it possible to separate relatively stable between-person differences in sleep from within-person deviations immediately preceding cognitive testing. Cognitive control was examined using a task-switching paradigm with bivalent stimuli. Flexibility was operationalised as the switch cost, defined as poorer performance on switch trials than repeat trials. Stability was operationalised as the congruency effect, defined as poorer performance on incongruent trials than congruent trials. Because the task crosses switch and congruency demands, it also allowed examination of whether sleep-related effects were strongest when flexibility and stability demands occurred together, as in switch-incongruent trials. Behavioural performance was measured using reaction time and accuracy, while frontal midline theta was examined as a neural index of control engagement. 

A unidimensional trade-off view would suggest that sleep-related changes in flexibility and stability should move in opposite directions. For example, if poor sleep increased switch costs by making the system less flexible, this could be accompanied by reduced congruency effects if control shifted toward stronger task-set maintenance. In contrast, a dual-dimensional view allows flexibility and stability to vary partly independently. From this perspective, poor sleep could be associated with increased switch costs, increased congruency effects, or both, depending on whether sleep primarily affects task-set updating, task-set shielding, or a broader control state. The present study examined whether naturalistic sleep variation was associated with switch-related effects, congruency-related effects, or their combination.


\paragraph{}
The following predictions were made for the current study:

\paragraph{}
Based on previous task-switching research, switch trials were expected to produce slower reaction times and lower accuracy than repeat trials, and incongruent trials were expected to produce slower reaction times and lower accuracy than congruent trials \parencite{rogers1995taskswitch,kiesel2010review}. Frontal midline theta was expected to increase under higher control demands, with stronger theta activity in switch trials and incongruent trials \parencite{cavanagh2014theta,cohen2013thetaconflict}.

\paragraph{}
The sleep-related predictions were guided by the idea that sleep affects some cognitive-control processes more strongly than others. Because sleep loss has been linked to reduced vigilance, slower processing, weaker task preparation, and greater task-set instability, shorter habitual sleep, greater sleep variability, and shorter-than-usual sleep before testing were expected to be associated with poorer behavioural performance. Based on previous sleep and task-switching studies, these effects were expected to be especially visible in switch costs, reflecting reduced flexibility or less efficient task-set reconfiguration \parencite{heuer2004totalsleepdeprivation,bratzke2009constantwakefulness,couyoumdjian2010switchingsleep,slama2018sleepswitching}. 

\paragraph{}
Sleep-related effects on congruency were also examined, because stability depends on maintaining the currently relevant task rule and shielding it from competing stimulus information. However, previous studies using bivalent task-switching paradigms suggest that sleep-related increases in switch costs are not necessarily explained by stronger congruency effects \parencite{heuer2004totalsleepdeprivation,bratzke2009constantwakefulness}. Therefore, effects on congruency were treated as theoretically plausible, but less strongly established than effects on switching.

\paragraph{}
Finally, sleep-related differences in frontal midline theta were examined to test whether naturalistic sleep variation was associated with neural control engagement. Because frontal midline theta is expected to increase when control demands are high, shorter habitual sleep, greater sleep variability, and shorter-than-usual sleep before testing were expected to be associated with reduced theta activity under demanding task conditions. Since previous research has more directly established behavioural sleep effects than theta-specific effects in naturalistic task switching, the comparison between Mean TST, TST variability, and TST deviation was treated as partly exploratory.

\section{Design and procedure}

\subsection{Ethical approval}
The project was approved by the Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics (REK \#197760).

\subsection{Data management}
In the Mind the Sleep project we collected information about health which is classified as sensitive data (Norwegian: særlige kategorier av personopplysninger). All data files generated as a part of MtS (i.e. EEG, MR, task performance, questionnaires, actigraphy) are stored on TSD.

\subsection{Study design}
The study was a within-subjects design with 3 sessions separated by 2 weeks. At the first session, participants were screened for depression using a a subset of the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview \parencite{sheehan1998mini}. They filled out baseline questionnaires, and were instructed in the use of a daily sleep diary. Participants reported their sleep continuously over four weeks. The two subsequent sessions involved EEG recordings during resting state and cognitive tasks assessing executive functions (stop-signal, task-switching, Stroop, and n-back).

\paragraph{Sample}
The project included adult participants (18-65 years), both with and without symptoms of depression. All participants were right-handed, spoke Norwegian, and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. All participants signed a written consent form before participating in the study. They were compensated with an 800 NOK gift card for their participation.

Participants were screened for depressive symptoms using the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) during a telephone interview before the first session \parencite{montgomery1979madrs}. Participants were excluded if they had a history of neurological or severe psychiatric disorders (e.g., psychotic disorders, bipolar disorder), significant head injury, or use of certain medications. Individuals with high depressive symptom scores (MADRS $>$ 20) were only included if they were receiving treatment for their depression.

The initial sample consisted of N = 50 participants. For sleep-related analyses, two participants were excluded because of missing sleep diaries, and two were excluded due to missing sleep data on the night prior to testing, leaving a sample of N = 46.


\subsection{Data acquisition}

We recorded EEG using a multichannel amplifier (BrainAmp, Brain Products GmbH, Germany) with 31 passive Ag/AgCl electrodes positioned according to the international 10–20 system. FCz served as the online reference and AFz as ground, with earlobe electrodes used for offline re-referencing. Electrode impedances were kept below 5 $k\Omega$. Data were recorded using BrainVision Recorder (Brain Products GmbH, Germany) at a sampling rate of 5000 Hz. Stimuli were presented on an Eizo FlexScan S2411W monitor (1920 \(\times\) 1200 resolution, 60 Hz refresh rate) at a viewing distance of approximately 70 cm. The experiment was run on a Dell Precision T5500 computer (Dell Inc., USA) using MATLAB (The MathWorks Inc., USA) with Psychophysics Toolbox 3. Participants used a Cedrus RB-740 response pad (Cedrus Corporation, San Pedro, USA) to respond to the tasks.

Each EEG session contained a resting-state recording (5 minutes with eyes open and 5 minutes with eyes closed) and four cognitive tasks administered in the following fixed order: stop-signal task, task-switching task, Stroop task, and n-back task \parencite{logan1984inhibition, rogers1995taskswitch, stroop1935interference, kirchner1958update}. The tasks lasted approximately 20, 15, 12, and 25 minutes, respectively. The order was kept constant across participants and sessions. The present thesis used data from the task-switching task recorded during the first EEG session.

\paragraph{Task-Switching Paradigm}
In the task-switching task (see figure \ref{fig:taskswitchingparadigm}), participants were presented with a pair of one letter and one number on the screen for 1200 ms. The font colour of the pair indicated which rule to follow, and was either blue or yellow (See table \ref{tab:task_switching} for response options). If the font colour was blue, then participants were instructed to report if the number was even (left button press) or odd (right button press). If the font colour was yellow, participants were instructed to report if the letter was a vowel (left button press) or a consonant (right button press). Prior to the presentation of the pair, a fixation cross was presented on the screen for a duration between 1300 and 1700 ms.

The trial types were either switch or repeat and either congruent or incongruent. In one-third of the trials, the font colour switched from the colour in the previous trial: the switch trials. In the remaining two-thirds of the trials, the font colour was the same as the previous trial: the repeat trials. Congruent trials were the ones where the different tasks would elicit the same correct response. For example, letter-number combinations of AEIYU and 02468 (vowels and even numbers) would be congruent whether the font colour was yellow or blue, because they would both elicit a left button press as the correct answer. Incongruent trials were the ones where participants would have to press different buttons for correct responses if the font colour changed, such as letter-number pairs consisting of e.g. AEIYU and 13579 (vowels and odd numbers), where the correct response for yellow font would be a left button press and correct response for blue font would be a right button press.
The task consisted of four blocks, with each block consisting of 60 trials. Participants had 15 trials for practicing before the experiment began. In total, the task consisted of 240 trials, adding up to a total duration of approximately 15 minutes. After each block, participants got feedback on their response. If their accuracy was less than 60\%, the message "Try to be more accurate" was displayed. With mean reaction times longer than 850 ms they got the message "Try to respond faster". If accuracy exceeded 60\% and mean reaction time was below 850 ms, they were shown the message "Well done". 

The behavioural outcome variables used for analysis in the task switching task were reaction times and accuracy. Reaction time was calculated from correct-response trials only. Incorrect trials and omission trials, defined as failures to respond within the 1200-ms response window, were excluded from reaction-time analyses. Accuracy was calculated as the proportion of successful trials, with both incorrect responses and omissions treated as unsuccessful trials.


\subsubsection{EEG data processing}

EEG preprocessing and time--frequency analyses were conducted in MATLAB using EEGLAB 2025.1.0 \parencite{delorme2004eeglab}, together with custom MATLAB scripts written for the present project. The scripts are openly available in the project code repository, described in Appendix~\ref{app:code}. 

For the preprocessing pipeline, I applied a low-pass filter at 40 Hz, resampled the data to 500 Hz, then applied a high-pass filter at 0.1 Hz and removed EMG channels. I then mapped electrode names and added FCz as the empty reference electrode, before re-referencing to the average of the earlobes. After that I ran an independent component analysis (ICA), and removed noisy components by manual inspection. Components were rejected when their scalp topography, time course, or spectral profile indicated non-neural activity such as eye movements, blinks, muscle activity, or channel noise. On average, 6.22 components were removed per participant (median = 6, range: 1--19). The upper end of this range reflected participants with pronounced ocular and muscle artifacts.

After component removal, I created an event list based on the logfiles, and renamed the triggers for epoching. Epochs from -1500 to 1500 ms around stimulus onset were extracted to prepare the data for time–frequency analysis. A longer window gives more stable estimates at lower frequencies, reduces edge problems, and leaves enough pre-stimulus data for baseline correction and normalisation. As theta is a low frequency oscillation, it requires a longer temporal window to be estimated reliably. Time-frequency estimates depend on having enough data around the event because edge artifacts can affect wavelet-based analysis \parencite{herrmann2014timefrequencytutorial}. Midfrontal electrodes Fz, FCz, and Cz were chosen as the region of analysis (ROI) because frontal midline theta in cognitive-control paradigms is typically maximal over the medial frontal scalp. I then inspected the ROI channels visually. I marked trials for rejection after epoching if electrodes exceeded physiological limits of ($\pm 200\,\mu V$), as recommended by \textcite{luck2014erp}. Restricting the rejection criterion to the electrodes of interest prevented noisy channels outside the ROI from causing unnecessary trial loss. Rejection was done only after the visual inspection.


\paragraph{Theta extraction and ERSP computation}
Time–frequency decomposition was performed in EEGLAB using the newtimef function on the full 3-second stimulus-locked epochs. Analyses were restricted to correct trials, and were conducted on condition-specific datasets corresponding to the four experimental conditions: repeat–congruent, repeat–incongruent, switch–congruent, and switch–incongruent. For each participant and condition, ERSPs were computed separately for the midfrontal electrodes Fz, FCz, and Cz using Morlet wavelets with parameter [3 0.8], a prestimulus baseline of $-500$ to $-100$ ms, an explicit initial window length of 1 s, and a frequency range of 3–25 Hz. ERSPs were then averaged across the three electrodes to get one time–frequency representation per participant and condition. Grand-average ERSPs were obtained by averaging these matrices across participants. Theta power was operationalised as mean ERSP power in the 4–8 Hz band from 200 to 500 ms after stimulus onset, averaged across time, frequency, and the three midfrontal electrodes, resulting in one summary value per participant and condition.

After rejection of flagged artifact epochs, baseline correction was applied using the interval from $-200$ to 0 ms. ERPs were then computed separately for the four experimental conditions (repeat--congruent, repeat--incongruent, switch--congruent, and switch--incongruent) by averaging across trials and across the midfrontal electrodes Fz, FCz, and Cz. Grand-average ERPs were obtained by averaging the resulting condition-specific waveforms across participants.


\subsection{Sleep diaries}
Sleep was assessed using daily entries in the Bergen Sleep Diary, completed continuously across four weeks between the first and subsequent laboratory sessions. Actigraphy was collected in parallel with sleep diaries, but was not used as a primary analytic measure in the present thesis. I used the actigraphy reports to verify the diary entries. The sleep variables were then calculated from the available diary entries, and nights without diary data were not included. The diaries were used as the primary source of sleep data in all analyses. Participants reported bedtime, time they attempted to fall asleep, sleep onset latency, nocturnal awakenings, and final morning awakening. From the available diary entries, nightly total sleep time (TST), sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset, sleep efficiency, and number of awakenings were derived. TST was calculated as the interval between attempted sleep time and final morning awakening, minus reported sleep onset latency and nocturnal wake time, measured in hours. 

The primary sleep variable used in the present analyses was diary-based total sleep time. Habitual sleep was operationalised as each participant's mean TST across valid diary nights in the 14-day period preceding the first EEG session. A valid diary night was defined as a retained diary entry for which TST could be calculated. In the sleep-analysis sample, participants contributed on average 12.52 valid nights (range: 9--14). Because the number of valid diary nights varied between participants, mean TST and TST variability were estimated with unequal precision across participants. Sleep variability was operationalised as each participant's standard deviation in TST across the same valid diary nights (TST SD). Nightly sleep deviation was calculated by centering each night's TST relative to the participant's own mean TST. For the present analyses, the relevant deviation score was the deviation from the night immediately preceding EEG testing (TST dev). Positive TST dev values reflect longer-than-usual sleep before testing, and negative values reflect shorter-than-usual sleep. 

\section{Statistical Analysis} 

I conducted statistical analyses to examine task-related effects in behavioural performance and frontal midline theta, as well as associations between sleep and behavioural and neural outcomes. Behavioural and EEG task-effect analyses were based on the full available sample (\(N = 50\)), whereas sleep-related analyses were restricted to participants with complete sleep and outcome data (\(N = 46\)).

Task effects were examined using repeated-measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs) with the within-subject factors Switch (repeat vs. switch) and Congruency (congruent vs. incongruent). Separate 2 \(\times\) 2 ANOVAs were conducted for reaction time, accuracy, and theta power. The main effect of Switch tested whether switch trials differed from repeat trials, corresponding to switch-related task demands. The main effect of Congruency tested whether incongruent trials differed from congruent trials, corresponding to interference-related task demands. The Switch \(\times\) Congruency interaction tested whether congruency effects differed between repeat and switch trials. When the Switch \(\times\) Congruency interaction was significant, I followed up the ANOVA with paired-samples simple-effects contrasts. These contrasts tested switch effects separately within congruent and incongruent trials, and congruency effects separately within repeat and switch trials. Follow-up contrasts were Holm-corrected within outcome.

Sleep-related hypotheses were tested using mixed-effects models with random intercepts for participant. Sleep-related models included one of three TST-based predictors at a time, Mean TST, TST SD, or TST dev. Separate models were fitted for reaction time, accuracy, and theta power. For reaction time and theta power, I used linear mixed-effects models. For accuracy, I used a generalised linear mixed-effects model with a binomial distribution and logit link. In the accuracy models, correct trials were coded as successes, and incorrect responses and omissions were coded as failures.

The same fixed-effect structure was used across the sleep-related models:

\[
Outcome \sim Switch \times Congruency \times Sleep + (1 \mid ID).
\]

The sleep-related terms were interpreted in relation to the study predictions. The main effect of Sleep tested whether the sleep predictor was associated with overall performance or theta power. The Sleep \(\times\) Switch interaction tested whether sleep was associated with switch-related effects, corresponding to flexibility demands. The Sleep \(\times\) Congruency interaction tested whether sleep was associated with congruency-related effects, corresponding to stability or interference-control demands. The Sleep \(\times\) Switch \(\times\) Congruency interaction tested whether sleep was associated with the combined demands of switching and incongruency.

For the models using Mean TST and TST SD, predictors were grand-mean centred prior to analysis. TST dev was already centred relative to each participant's own mean by definition. Because logistic mixed-effects model coefficients are expressed on the log-odds scale, model-predicted probabilities were calculated for nominal accuracy interactions to aid interpretation. Equivalent prediction tables were not calculated for reaction time or theta power because these outcomes were modelled on continuous scales and could be interpreted directly in seconds or ERSP power.

According to a two-tailed sensitivity analysis in G*Power (\(\alpha = .05\), power = 0.80), the sleep-related analysis sample had sufficient power to detect moderate associations (\(r = .40\)), but not small effects. Statistical significance was evaluated at \(\alpha = .05\). All tests were two-sided. Although some predictions were directional, two-sided tests were retained as a conservative approach. Multiple-comparison correction was applied within hypothesis-relevant families of sleep effects. Sleep main effects, Sleep \(\times\) Switch interactions, Sleep \(\times\) Congruency interactions, and Sleep \(\times\) Switch \(\times\) Congruency interactions were treated as separate families. Holm--Bonferroni correction was applied within each family to control the family-wise error rate. Both uncorrected \(p\)-values and Holm-corrected \(p\)-values are reported where relevant.



\chapter{Results}

\section{Sample characteristics}

The full sample consisted of 50 participants (\(M_{age} = 39.62\), \(SD = 10.18\)). 10 were men and 40 were women. Behavioural and EEG task-effect analyses were based on the full sample. Sleep descriptives and sleep-related mixed-effects models were based on participants with complete sleep and outcome data (\(N = 46\)).

\subsection{Behavioural performance}

Participants were accurate overall (\(\sim 71.94\%\) correct), with lower performance on switch than repeat trials, and on incongruent than congruent trials (See figure \ref{fig:barchart_accuracy}). Accuracy by condition showed that correct responses were highest in repeat--congruent trials (82.41\%) and lowest in switch--incongruent trials (51.08\%). Omissions were more frequent in switch than repeat trials.

Reaction times were longer on switch trials (M = 866.72) ms, (SD = 194.07) than on repeat trials (M = 729.71) ms, (SD = 53.59). Mean reaction times were slightly longer on incongruent trials (M = 776.36 ms, SD = 46.54) than on congruent trials (M = 766.34 ms, SD = 51.16). Condition-wise means showed the slowest correct responses in switch--incongruent trials (M = 911.09) ms, (SD = 60.03) and the fastest in repeat--congruent trials (M = 724.67) ms, (SD = 57.50).

A 2 \(\times\) 2 repeated-measures ANOVA on reaction time showed a significant main effect of switch, \(F(1,49) = 394.50\), \(p < .001\), a significant main effect of congruency, \(F(1,49) = 24.77\), \(p < .001\), and a significant switch \(\times\) congruency interaction, \(F(1,49) = 10.19\), \(p = .002\). Because the interaction was significant, I followed up the ANOVA with paired-samples simple-effects contrasts, Holm-corrected within outcome. Switch costs were significant in both congruent trials, \(M_{\mathrm{diff}} = 148\) ms, \(t(49) = 17.07\), \(p_{\mathrm{Holm}} < .001\), and incongruent trials, \(M_{\mathrm{diff}} = 176\) ms, \(t(49) = 18.09\), \(p_{\mathrm{Holm}} < .001\). Congruency effects were also significant in both repeat trials, \(M_{\mathrm{diff}} = 11\) ms, \(t(49) = 2.38\), \(p_{\mathrm{Holm}} = .021\), and switch trials, \(M_{\mathrm{diff}} = 38\) ms, \(t(49) = 4.72\), \(p_{\mathrm{Holm}} < .001\). Responses were slower on switch than repeat trials and slower on incongruent than congruent trials, with the congruency effect being larger on switch trials.

A corresponding ANOVA on accuracy also revealed a significant main effect of switch, \(F(1,49) = 296.79\), \(p < .001\), a significant main effect of congruency, \(F(1,49) = 68.08\), \(p < .001\), and a significant switch \(\times\) congruency interaction, \(F(1,49) = 13.66\), \(p < .001\). Follow-up paired-samples simple-effects contrasts showed significant switch costs in both congruent trials, \(M_{\mathrm{diff}} = -18.84\) percentage points, \(t(49) = -11.43\), \(p_{\mathrm{Holm}} < .001\), and incongruent trials, \(M_{\mathrm{diff}} = -25.10\) percentage points, \(t(49) = -17.87\), \(p_{\mathrm{Holm}} < .001\). Congruency effects were also significant in both repeat trials, \(M_{\mathrm{diff}} = -6.23\) percentage points, \(t(49) = -4.95\), \(p_{\mathrm{Holm}} < .001\), and switch trials, \(M_{\mathrm{diff}} = -12.50\) percentage points, \(t(49) = -8.01\), \(p_{\mathrm{Holm}} < .001\). Negative mean differences indicate lower accuracy in the more demanding condition. Accuracy was lower on switch than repeat trials and lower on incongruent than congruent trials, with the congruency effect being larger on switch trials.

\subsection{Reaction time}

Three mixed-effects models were fitted for reaction time, with TST dev, Mean TST, and TST SD entered in separate models. In the TST dev model, the main effect of TST dev was not significant, \(b = 0.019\), \(SE = 0.010\), \(t = 1.80\), \(p = .074\). The TST dev \(\times\) switch interaction was nominally significant, \(b = -0.022\), \(SE = 0.010\), \(t = -2.12\), \(p = .035\), indicating that higher TST dev was associated with reduced switch-related slowing. Because reaction time was modelled in seconds, this estimate corresponds to approximately 22 ms less switch-related slowing for each additional hour of sleep relative to the participant's own mean. The TST dev \(\times\) congruency interaction was not significant, \(b = -0.010\), \(SE = 0.010\), \(t = -0.93\), \(p = .354\), and the switch \(\times\) congruency \(\times\) TST dev interaction was not significant, \(b = 0.012\), \(SE = 0.015\), \(t = 0.84\), \(p = .402\).

In the Mean TST model, the main effect of Mean TST was not significant, \(b = -0.013\), \(SE = 0.010\), \(t = -1.32\), \(p = .189\). Mean TST did not significantly interact with switch, \(b = 0.008\), \(SE = 0.010\), \(t = 0.79\), \(p = .433\), or congruency, \(b = -0.007\), \(SE = 0.010\), \(t = -0.67\), \(p = .505\). The switch \(\times\) congruency \(\times\) Mean TST interaction was also not significant, \(b = 0.011\), \(SE = 0.014\), \(t = 0.75\), \(p = .452\).

In the TST SD model, the main effect of TST SD was not significant, \(b = 0.039\), \(SE = 0.022\), \(t = 1.76\), \(p = .080\). The TST SD \(\times\) switch interaction was nominally significant, \(b = -0.046\), \(SE = 0.022\), \(t = -2.08\), \(p = .039\), indicating that higher TST SD was associated with reduced switch-related slowing. This corresponds to approximately 46 ms less switch-related slowing per additional hour of TST SD. The TST SD \(\times\) congruency interaction was not significant, \(b = -0.007\), \(SE = 0.022\), \(t = -0.32\), \(p = .747\), and the switch \(\times\) congruency \(\times\) TST SD interaction was not significant, \(b = 0.026\), \(SE = 0.031\), \(t = 0.85\), \(p = .398\).

\chapter{Discussion}
The purpose of the present study was to investigate the relationship between sleep and executive functions, and more specifically whether habitual or nightly sleep influences cognitive flexibility. The thesis also investigates whether these sleep mechanisms differentiate between a unidimensional or a dual-dimensional view of set shifting, by trying to tease cognitive flexibility and stability apart. 

Based on previous studies showing that sleep deprivation disproportionately affects sustained attention and goal maintenance, I hypothesised that poorer sleep would be associated with larger congruency effects, reflecting reduced cognitive stability. The task itself produced clear congruency effects, with incongruent trials being slower and less accurate than congruent trials. However, none of the models reached statistical significance for any of the congruency-related sleep interactions. In other words, the study found evidence that the congruency manipulation worked behaviourally, but not that poorer sleep amplified congruency costs.
The second hypothesis was that association between sleep and switch costs should be weaker or less consistent than the sleep-congruency effects, because previous studies have shown that basic processes such as response inhibition and conflict detection appear relatively preserved after sleep deprivation. This would reflect a smaller effect of sleep on cognitive flexibility. This was partly contradicted by the results. The task produced very clear switch costs in both reaction time and accuracy, and the sleep-related effects appeared more often for switch-related outcomes than for congruency-related outcomes. Longer-than-usual sleep before EEG showed nominal associations with reduced switch-related slowing in reaction time, and greater sleep variability was also nominally associated with reduced switch-related slowing. In accuracy, both habitual sleep duration and sleep variability interacted with switch condition. Rather than sleep effects being mainly tied to stability, the nominal results in this thesis pointed to sleep being consistently related to switch-related processes. Frontal midline theta was expected to increase with task demands, with higher theta in incongruent and switch trials, and poorer sleep was expected to be associated with lower theta overall. This hypothesis was not supported. Descriptively, theta was highest in the switch-congruent condition, but the ANOVA showed no significant main effect of switch, no significant main effect of congruency, and no switch × congruency interaction. Unlike the behavioural outcomes, theta did not show reliable task-related modulation. Mean total sleep time and sleep variability did not show significant main effects on theta, but both showed switch-related interactions before correction. No congruency-related theta interactions were significant. As such, theta did not behave as the expected general marker of reduced control recruitment under poorer sleep, and the only sleep-theta associations were tied to switching.

The present thesis found behavioural evidence that the task captured both flexibility and stability demands, but the sleep hypotheses were not supported in the expected direction. Sleep did not mainly affect congruency effects, and theta did not reliably increase under higher task demands. The most coherent pattern was that the nominal sleep-related effects were concentrated around switch-related performance and switch-related theta variation, while congruency-related sleep effects were largely absent. In this sample, sleep was not a clear predictor of cognitive stability or flexibility, and frontal midline theta did not provide evidence for the proposed neural mechanism.



\section{Stability and flexibility}

THE DEBATE:
\parencite{dreisbach2024dependent,egner2024reply}
Recent correspondence has further clarified that the disagreement may not be whether stability and flexibility are always dependent or always independent, but at which mechanistic level dependence should be expected. Dreisbach, Musslick, and Braem argue that stability–flexibility trade-offs may arise at specific levels of processing, whereas Egner argues that assuming reciprocity in advance makes the trade-off difficult to test empirically. This debate is relevant to the present thesis because switch costs and congruency effects were treated as related but not interchangeable indices of cognitive control.”


The clearest behavioural finding was that the task produced robust switch costs and congruency effects. Participants were slower and less accurate on switch trials than repeat trials, and less accurate on incongruent trials than congruent trials. This suggests that the task successfully captured both components targeted in the thesis: flexibility, operationalised as switch costs, and stability, operationalised as congruency effects. The sleep-related results, however, did not follow the expected pattern. Based on previous sleep deprivation research, I expected poorer sleep to be more strongly associated with congruency effects, reflecting reduced stability or poorer shielding of the active task set. Instead, the nominal sleep-related effects were concentrated around switch-related outcomes, whereas congruency-related sleep interactions were largely absent. This pattern should be interpreted cautiously, because none of the sleep-related effects survived correction. Still, the distribution of the nominal effects is informative for the theoretical question. Switch costs are commonly interpreted as reflecting the demands of changing from one task set to another, including task-set reconfiguration, interference from the previously relevant rule, and preparation for the currently relevant response mapping \parencite{rogers1995taskswitch,monsell2003switching,kiesel2010review}. In the present study, sleep variables showed nominal interactions with switching for reaction time, accuracy, and theta, but not with congruency. This may suggest that, in this sample and task context, naturalistic variation in sleep was more closely related to processes involved in updating or reconfiguring task sets than to processes involved in shielding the current task set from interference.

The absence of congruency-related sleep effects is also important. Congruency effects were clearly present behaviourally, indicating that incongruent trials were more demanding than congruent trials, but the effects did not seem to be reliably modulated by sleep. This does not necessarily mean that cognitive stability is unaffected by sleep. It could suggest that the degree of naturalistic sleep variation observed here was not sufficient to produce detectable changes in congruency-related performance. One possibility is that congruency effects in this task rely partly on relatively robust stimulus--response competition processes, whereas switching depends more strongly on transient control processes involved in updating task rules. If so, switch costs may be more sensitive to fluctuations in arousal, fatigue, or control engagement than the congruency effect measured here. This interpretation aligns partly with the literature on sleep deprivation. Previous studies suggest that sleep loss affects sustained attention, goal maintenance, and effortful top-down control, while some more automatic control processes appear relatively preserved \parencite{kusztor2019differentialsleep}. I originally expected this to produce stronger associations between poor sleep and congruency effects, because stability depends on maintaining task goals and resisting interference. The present findings did not support that prediction. Instead, the nominal effects suggest that sleep may have been more relevant for the flexible updating of task sets than for interference control. This could mean that naturalistic sleep variation does not map neatly onto findings from acute sleep deprivation studies. It is also possible that flexibility and stability are affected differently depending on whether sleep is manipulated experimentally or measured as an everyday, naturally varying behaviour.
The results in the present study do not provide strong evidence that sleep selectively affects either flexibility or stability, but they do suggest a tentative pattern in which sleep-related variation was more visible for switch-related outcomes than for congruency-related outcomes. This could reflect the context-sensitive aspect of cognitive control, where performance varies both with stable executive ability and with current state factors such as sleep, arousal, and task engagement. Since the sleep-related effects were not robust enough to survive correction for multiple testing, it could mean that we would need a larger sample of participants to see if the trends reflect real sleep effects on cognitive control.

A possible mechanistic way to interpret the concentration of nominal sleep-related effects around switching is through state-dependent variation in task-set updating or gating. Task switching requires participants to disengage from the previously relevant task set and configure the currently relevant rule. In the present task, this meant updating whether the stimulus should be classified according to the number or the letter. Switch costs could then reflect task-set reconfiguration, interference from the previous rule, and the updating of stimulus--response mappings \parencite{rogers1995taskswitch,monsell2003switching,kiesel2010review}. Within frontostriatal gating accounts, prefrontal regions are thought to support the active maintenance of task goals, whereas striatal gating mechanisms regulate when new information is allowed to update the current task set \parencite{cools2019gatingmechanisms}. From this perspective, switch trials may place stronger demands on transient updating and gating than repeat trials. If naturalistic sleep variation affects arousal, fatigue, or the efficiency with which control is recruited, these effects may be more visible in switch-related outcomes than in congruency effects. In contrast, congruency effects may partly reflect stimulus--response competition within the currently active task set, which may have been less sensitive to the range of sleep variation observed in this study. Since we did not measure frontostriatal activity directly, and none of the sleep-related fixed effects survived correction, the findings in this thesis cannot be taken as evidence for sleep modulated frontostriatal gating. But the pattern provides a possible theoretical link between the observed concentration of nominal switch-related effects and models that distinguish between maintaining a task set and updating it when demands change.




\section{Habitual sleep and variability}

The sleep-related findings also raise the question of why habitual sleep duration and sleep variability appeared to show different patterns. Habitual sleep (mean TST) and sleep variation (TST SD) were included to capture different aspects of sleep. Habitual sleep was reflected in how much a participant typically slept across the recording period, and sleep variability captured how much sleep duration varied from night to night. The values of these variables cannot be universally interpreted as interchangeable indicators of ``better'' or ``worse'' sleep. Habitual sleep duration is meant to reflect relatively stable individual differences in sleep need, lifestyle, chronotype, or baseline arousal, while sleep variability reflects instability in sleep routines, irregular schedules, or flexibility in sleep behaviour. This distinction is consistent with the idea that individual sleep needs are not fixed around one universal optimum, but vary across individuals and contexts \parencite{fjell2024sleep}. In the present study, the nominal effects of habitual sleep duration and habitual sleep variability pointed in different directions for switch-related accuracy and theta. Higher mean total sleep time was associated with a larger switch-related reduction in accuracy, but also with a larger switch-related theta difference. Higher sleep variability, on the other hand, was associated with a smaller switch-related reduction in accuracy and a smaller switch-related theta difference. Because these effects did not survive correction, they should not be interpreted as strong evidence for opposite effects of habitual sleep and sleep variability. Still, the pattern suggests that average sleep duration and sleep variability may capture partly different participant characteristics. A possible interpretation of these effects is that habitual sleep reflects relatively stable individual differences in sleep need or cognitive strategy. Participants with longer total sleep time on average may not necessarily be ``better rested'' than participants with shorter habitual sleep. They may simply need more sleep to reach a comparable level of functioning. If so, longer mean total sleep time could mark a subgroup with different baseline arousal, fatigue vulnerability, or task strategy. The combination of larger switch-related theta and larger switch-related accuracy costs could be interpreted as greater control recruitment under switching demands, but not necessarily more efficient performance. In this view, higher theta may reflect increased effort or compensatory engagement rather than better control.

A related possibility is that the sleep-related pattern reflects differences in speed--accuracy strategy. Decision-making models describe performance as depending partly on how much evidence is accumulated before a response is made. A more cautious response strategy increases the amount of evidence required before responding, whereas a less cautious strategy allows faster but potentially more error-prone responding \parencite{bogacz2010speedaccuracy}. Although the present study did not model latent decision parameters directly, this framework provides a possible interpretation of the behavioural findings. Participants with longer habitual sleep may have adopted a strategy involving greater control engagement during switch trials, reflected in higher switch-related theta, while still showing larger accuracy costs under switching demands. Conversely, participants with more variable sleep may have shown smaller measured switch costs because of a different response strategy or lower phasic control engagement, rather than because sleep variability was beneficial. This interpretation is speculative since the analyses that were carried out in this thesis cannot distinguish between sleep need, sleep quality, chronotype, lifestyle regularity, motivation, or response strategy. The sleep measures were based on diaries, and diary-based total sleep time could partly reflect subjective reporting habits in addition to actual sleep duration. Unmeasured variables such as time of day, caffeine use, work schedules, mood, or depressive symptoms could also contribute to the observed associations. In addition, executive-function task measures are known to be influenced by both trait-like and state-like factors, and common behavioural indices of control may show limited temporal stability \parencite{thunberg2024unreliability}. The apparent differences between mean total sleep time and sleep variability should be interpreted as a possible pattern to follow up, and not as evidence that habitual sleep duration and sleep variability have opposite causal effects on cognitive flexibility. The present analyses cannot determine whether these patterns reflect cognitive control differences, response strategies, or broader state factors such as arousal and task engagement. Further analytic approaches could be pursued to separate stable individual differences from occasion-specific variation, and behavioural model approaches could separate accuracy, speed, and response caution more directly.



\section{Midfrontal theta}

Frontal midline theta was included as a neural marker of control engagement, based on previous work linking theta activity to conflict monitoring, task demands, and the need for cognitive control \parencite{cavanagh2014theta,cohen2013thetaconflict}. In the present study theta did not show the expected task-related pattern. The repeated-measures ANOVA showed no significant main effect of switch, no significant main effect of congruency, and no switch \(\times\) congruency interaction. This means that theta did not function as a clear general marker of task demand in the present analysis, even though the behavioural task effects were robust. There are several possible reasons for this null result. Theta power varied substantially between participants, especially in the switch conditions, which may have reduced sensitivity to condition-level differences. The number of usable trials also varied by condition, with incongruent switch trials being the lowest. This could have made theta estimates less stable. The theta measure used in the present thesis was also a relatively broad summary measure, defined as mean 4--8 Hz power from 200--500 ms across Fz, FCz, and Cz. This window was theoretically motivated, but individual differences in the timing, frequency, or scalp distribution of theta responses could have blurred condition effects when averaged across participants. The absence of significant theta task effects does not necessarily mean that theta was irrelevant to task performance, but it does mean that the present analysis did not provide strong evidence for the expected task-related theta modulation.

The sleep-related theta models showed a different, but still tentative, pattern. Habitual sleep and sleep variability showed nominal switch \(\times\) sleep interactions, but sleep did not interact with congruency. Longer habitual sleep durations tended to be associated with a larger theta increase during switch trials. When participants’ sleep duration varied more from night to night, theta increased less on switch trials compared with repeat trials. These nominal effects mirror the broader pattern in which sleep-related associations were concentrated around switch-related outcomes and not with congruency-related outcomes. A possible interpretation is that the switch-related theta findings reflect differences in control engagement. Frontal midline theta has been proposed to signal the need for control and to support coordination of control across task-relevant networks \parencite{cavanagh2014theta,cavanagh2015metatheta}. From this perspective, the larger switch-related theta difference when participants had longer habitual sleep could reflect greater recruitment of control during switch trials. Increased theta can reflect efficient control engagement, but it can also reflect greater effort or greater experienced demand, so more theta activity does not automatically translate to better performance. At the group level, the nominal associations showed that higher habitual sleep duration was linked to larger switch-related theta differences, but not to better switch-related accuracy. This could mean that theta reflected effort, task demand, or compensatory engagement and not more efficient cognitive control. Higher sleep variability was associated with a smaller switch-related theta difference, while also being associated with smaller switch-related accuracy costs. This could reflect reduced phasic control engagement, a different response strategy, or lower sensitivity of the theta measure in participants with more variable sleep. It does not necessarily mean that sleep variability improves cognitive control. Rather, it suggests that sleep variability and habitual sleep duration may relate differently to neural and behavioural indices of switching, and that theta alone is not sufficient to determine whether these patterns reflect better control, greater effort, or strategic differences in task performance.

Overall, the theta findings were weaker than expected. Behaviourally, the task clearly elicited switch and congruency effects, but theta did not show reliable task-related modulation. The nominal sleep-related theta interactions suggest a possible link between habitual sleep patterns and switch-related control engagement, but this interpretation remains tentative. Future work could test this more directly by modelling both EEG sessions while accounting for session and practice effects, and by exploring whether participant-specific theta windows provide more sensitive estimates of frontal midline theta.

\section{Unitary or dual-dimensional cognitive control?}

One of the broader aims of this thesis was to examine whether sleep-related variation would support a unitary or a more differentiated view of cognitive control. A strictly unitary account would predict that a contextual factor such as sleep would affect cognitive control more globally, producing similar associations with switch costs, congruency effects, and theta activity. In a dual-dimensional account, flexibility and stability can vary partly independently. From this perspective, sleep could be associated with one component of control without necessarily affecting the other. The present results cannot rule out one account or the other, but it might fit more closely with a dual-dimensional framework, although only tentatively. 

Behaviourally, the task produced both switch costs and congruency effects, showing that both flexibility and stability demands were present. At the same time, the nominal sleep-related effects were concentrated around switch-related outcomes, while congruency-related sleep interactions were largely absent. This suggests that naturalistic sleep variation may have been more closely related to processes involved in task-set updating or reconfiguration than to interference control. In this sense, the findings are more consistent with a view of cognitive control as composed of partly separable processes than with a fully unitary control construct. At the same time, there are several arguments against the support for a dual-dimensional framework that can be drawn from the results. None of the sleep-related effects survived correction. The study also used behavioural proxies for flexibility and stability rather than direct measures of the underlying mechanisms. When operationalizing cognitive mechanisms we try to tease apart behaviour to reach appropriate levels of explanation, but making a task process-pure is difficult. Switch costs can reflect preparation, task-set reconfiguration, and interference from the previous task, while congruency effects can reflect several forms of stimulus--response competition. The findings in this thesis show that sleep-related variation did not affect all control measures uniformly, but they do not establish that flexibility and stability are fully independent systems.

What the results do support is the broader idea that cognitive control is context-sensitive. Task performance appeared to depend both on the experimental manipulation itself, and on individual differences in sleep patterns, and possibly on state-dependent factors such as arousal, effort, and strategy. This fits with accounts of executive function in which stable control abilities coexist with task-specific and occasion-specific variation \parencite{friedman2017unity}. In this sense, the present thesis does not reject a unity-and-diversity view of executive function. Rather, it adds to the argument that single-task performance reflects both common control demands and more specific processes that may be differentially sensitive to contextual factors such as sleep.




\section{Limitations and future research}

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the sleep-related analyses were based on a modest sample size (\(N = 46\)). The sensitivity analysis indicated that this sample was sufficient to detect moderate bivariate associations, but not small effects. The main sleep-related hypotheses were tested as interaction effects in mixed-effects models, which generally require more power than main effects. As mentioned before, none of the sleep-related fixed effects survived correction, and the nominal switch-related sleep effects should be interpreted as tentative patterns rather than robust evidence that sleep modulated cognitive control. The absence of congruency-related sleep effects should also not be interpreted as strong evidence that sleep is unrelated to stability, but rather as evidence that no reliable association was detected in the present sample.

A second limitation concerns the use of only one EEG session in the present thesis. The broader Mind the Sleep project included two EEG sessions, but the current analysis focused on the first task-switching EEG session. This was a deliberate scope decision, because including both sessions would introduce additional sources of variance, including session order, practice effects, time between sessions, and possible changes in EEG data quality. Using only one EEG session limited the ability to test within-person sleep effects. TST deviation was defined relative to each participant's own average sleep duration, but because task performance and theta were analysed from a single session, the model could not test whether a participant performed differently on nights after sleeping more or less than usual. Future analyses could extend the present work by modelling both EEG sessions while explicitly accounting for session and practice effects. This would allow a stronger test of whether night-before sleep deviations are associated with corresponding within-person changes in reaction time, accuracy, or theta. The limitation of using only one EEG session is also relevant for the broader distinction between stable individual differences and state-dependent variation. A central idea in the thesis is that cognitive control may reflect both relatively stable abilities and context-sensitive fluctuations. With the current design I could not fully separate these sources of variance. Latent state--trait modelling would be one possible framework for this question, because it is designed to separate stable trait variance from occasion-specific state variance and measurement error \parencite{steyer1999lst}. With only two EEG sessions, however, a model like this would be limited because it could estimate stable between-person differences and occasion-specific residual variance, but it would not be able to cleanly separate trait, state, and error components in the way that would be possible with three or more measurement occasions. Future studies with at least three EEG sessions would therefore be better suited for testing whether sleep-related variation in cognitive control reflects stable individual differences, temporary states, or measurement noise.

The sleep measures also have limitations. Sleep was assessed using daily sleep diaries, with actigraphy used to verify diary entries and identify implausible sleep periods. This approach allowed sleep to be studied across several weeks in a relatively naturalistic setting, but diary-based TST is still partly subjective. Participants may differ in how accurately they estimate sleep onset, awakenings, and final awakening, and diary-based sleep may not correspond perfectly to actigraphy or polysomnography. This is important because mean TST and TST SD do reflect sleep duration and variability, but they could also reflect reporting habits, lifestyle, chronotype, mood, or daily schedule. Future work should test whether the present pattern holds when using actigraphy-derived sleep variables, and whether diary and actigraphy measures explain overlapping or different aspects of cognitive performance. A more detailed sleep design could also examine whether specific sleep features, such as sleep efficiency, sleep fragmentation, slow-wave sleep, or REM sleep, are more closely related to cognitive control than total sleep time alone.

Longitudinal design: Poor sleep quality was associated with greater cortical atrophy over time in widespread frontal, temporal, and parietal regions, although the authors emphasized that the design could not establish whether poor sleep caused brain change or reflected ongoing brain change \parencite{sexton2014sleepatrophy}. Longitudinal studies improve temporal inference, but they do not fully solve causal direction, because sleep problems may be both a risk factor for later mental health problems and a consequence of them \parencite{alvaro2013sleepdepression, sivertsen2012bidirectional}.

There are some limitations related to the use of actigraphy for sleep measurement. Polysomnography (PSG) remains the gold standard for assessing sleep because it provides a direct physiological measurement of sleep stages through simultaneous recording of brain activity (EEG), eye movements (EOG), and muscle tone (EMG), allowing precise differentiation between sleep and wakefulness. PSG requires an extensive set-up, which makes it more suitable for lab studies. Wrist actigraphy is a practical and validated alternative for field studies where we are interested in habitual sleep. In the present study, only the accelerometer function of the ActiGraph devices was used, with all other sensors disabled to preserve battery life and participants’ privacy. Because actigraphy infers sleep and wake from movement alone, it may misclassify periods of quiet wakefulness as sleep, and can underestimate wake after sleep onset (WASO) compared with PSG. To ensure accuracy, actigraphy data were cross-checked against participants’ sleep diaries. Any apparent sleep periods not confirmed in the diary were excluded from analyses. Sources of errors here could be when participants take the actigraph off and leave it on the counter for periods of the day. The stillness will sometimes be registered as sleep.

Another limitation concerns the measurement of cognitive control. The task-switching paradigm allowed flexibility and stability to be examined within the same task, using switch costs and congruency effects as behavioural indices. As already mentioned, these measures are not process-pure. Switch costs may reflect task-set reconfiguration, preparation, interference from the previous task, response selection, and general slowing, while congruency effects may reflect several forms of stimulus--response competition \parencite{rogers1995taskswitch,kiesel2010review}. This means that the present results cannot identify the exact mechanism behind the nominal sleep-related switch effects. A smaller switch cost could reflect more efficient updating, but it could also reflect differences in response caution, motivation, or speed--accuracy strategy. Future work could address these questions by combining task-switching data with computational modelling. Drift-diffusion modelling could help separate response caution, information accumulation, and non-decision processes \parencite{bogacz2010speedaccuracy}. This would make it possible to test whether sleep variables are related to cognitive control itself, or whether the observed associations are better explained by differences in response strategy, arousal, or task engagement. Such modelling could be useful for interpreting the opposite nominal patterns observed for mean TST and TST SD in accuracy and theta.

FROM THE INTRO -LATENT STATE VS TRAIT EXPLAINED: Since executive functions like inhibition, updating, and shifting are theoretical constructs, they cannot be measured directly. In their unity-diversity article, \textcite{friedman2017unity} reviewed individual-differences studies that used latent-variable models to examine whether executive-function tasks reflect one common construct or several separable but related components. The assumption in latent-variable approaches is that individual executive-function tasks are not process-pure. For example, a Stroop task, stop-signal task, and antisaccade task may all require inhibition, but they also differ in perceptual demands, response format, stimulus processing, and strategy use. Latent-variable analysis uses the covariance among several tasks to estimate the component that is shared across them. Because random error and task-specific demands are less likely to be shared across different tasks, the latent factor is interpreted as a cleaner estimate of the underlying executive function than any single task score. In the unity–diversity model, tasks designed to measure inhibition, updating, and shifting clustered into separable but correlated latent factors. This means that the executive functions could not be reduced to a single common factor, but they were also not independent. While latent-variable models are used to extract stable latent factors, latent state-trait theory uses repeated within-subject observations to tease apart trait-like variance, occasion-specific situational influences and noise \parencite{steyer1999lst}. The basic logic is that a single observed score can first be decomposed into a latent state score and measurement error. The latent state score can then be decomposed further into a stable trait component and a state residual, where the residual captures situational influences and person-by-situation interactions. In this framework, stable differences are inferred from what is consistent across occasions, whereas state-like effects are inferred from systematic deviations that are specific to a given occasion. 

Finally, the generalisability of the findings is limited by the sample and design. The sample consisted of adults with and without depressive symptoms, and the sex distribution was uneven. Potentially relevant factors such as depressive symptoms, age, sex, time of day, caffeine use, work schedule, chronotype, and trial counts were not included as covariates in the present models. These factors could influence both sleep and cognitive performance, and may partly account for the observed associations. Future studies could use larger samples and model relevant covariates more explicitly. Experimental sleep manipulation, such as within-subject sleep restriction or sleep extension, would also be needed to test causal effects of sleep on switching, congruency effects, and theta. The present findings should therefore be seen as a naturalistic first step: they suggest possible links between sleep patterns and switch-related cognitive control, but stronger designs are needed to determine whether these associations are reliable, causal, and mechanistically specific.



\chapter{Conclusion}

This thesis set out to examine whether everyday sleep variation helps explain differences in cognitive control, and whether these associations differ between flexibility and stability. The behavioural task effects were clear, with participants showing robust costs of both switching and incongruency. This means that the task successfully captured the control demands it was meant to capture. The sleep-related findings, however, were more tentative. Sleep did not show the expected pattern of stronger associations with congruency effects, and frontal midline theta did not show reliable task-related modulation. Counter to the expected findings, the nominal sleep-related effects were concentrated around switching rather than congruency. Even though the thesis did not provide strong evidence for either a unitary or dual-dimensional understanding of cognitive control, the concentration of effects around switching does point more in the direction of a differentiated view rather than a fully global sleep effect.

The thesis supports the value of studying sleep and cognitive control in a naturalistic framework, but also shows the difficulty of doing so. Everyday sleep is variable, cognitive-control measures are not process-pure, and neural markers such as frontal midline theta may not map cleanly onto behaviour in modest samples. Even so, the null findings contribute to the understanding that the everyday relationship between sleep and cognitive control is complex, and may differ from what we find in more controlled experiments. The central question of the thesis remains open. Future work is needed to test whether naturalistic sleep variation is relevant for task-set updating and flexible control, and whether this can be separated from response strategy, arousal, and individual sleep need.

\chapter{Appendix C: Prompt history for AI-generated conceptual illustration}
\label{app:controlstates}

The conceptual illustration in Figure~\ref{fig:controlstates} was generated using ChatGPT. I designed the concept, specified the four cognitive-control states, and iteratively revised the image through follow-up prompts. The final figure was produced after a sequence of prompts requesting a 2 \(\times\) 2 black-and-white manga-style kitchen scene with the same protagonist across panels, where each panel illustrated a different combination of cognitive stability and flexibility.

The final prompt sequence specified that the image should show: 
(1) high stability/low flexibility, where the protagonist remains focused on chopping carrots while the pot begins to boil over without her noticing; 
(2) high flexibility/low stability, where she is distracted by multiple stimuli, looks toward her phone, and lifts the pot lid, but the pot does not boil over;
